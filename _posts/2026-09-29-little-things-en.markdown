This is more of a personal story about what I'm going through day to day, and about the people around me: my relationships, my family, everything else.

It comes down to an attitude. I won't say holding on to it, but actually doing, every day, the small things you hear about in so many podcasts and YouTube videos: do this, do that. Even *Atomic Habits* (James Clear, I had forgotten the author's name) is built on the same idea: lots of small things.

Very often, starting a small habit comes with the thought "yes, let's start, in order to reach a final goal." There is always a goal behind it. In this post I want to put three ideas next to that:

1. **It's the small things that matter, the gesture, more than the big ones.**
2. **Fall in love with the process, not with the final goal.**
3. **Consistency beats intensity:** doing something regularly is more important than doing it all in one go.

## 1. The small things

I'm thinking of writing to a person, getting in touch with a client at work, sending something. The older you get, the more the world fills up. I won't say it gets complicated, but you simply have so many things to do and to keep in mind that many of them slip through.

Most of the acquaintances and friendships you make over a lifetime fade away not because someone treated you badly or fell out with you, but simply because you forgot about them. Not on purpose: you didn't have time, you had too much on your mind. That's how most connections fade, because people are too busy.

Many people think "does this person think of me?", but people already have enough problems of their own. They are busy thinking about next month, the next investment, the other thing.

Even the beautiful finale of *Scrubs* touches on this: for all the people you've met, whether you made them happy, made them smile or made them cry, that already counts for something. A moment fades and is gone right away. So even hearing someone say "I care about you, let's catch up, let's go out" is something distinctive, because few people do it. If someone does it with you, it's a person who took the time to think of you and write to you, and very often it costs just 10, 15, 20 seconds.

I'm a victim of this myself. But I try my best to do it, even the small things.

## 2. The process, not the goal

Someone starts going to the gym to lose 10 kg. Someone else goes because they want to be a bit more muscular to make a better impression on a girl. Whatever the reason, if it works for you, that's perfectly fine.

But I've noticed there is often an expectation that eats at you, a voice in your head saying "I did too much of this," or "today I didn't do it," and you punish yourself. It becomes a sort of torture, a judge telling you "this is okay, that isn't", and it takes you further away from the goal you wanted to reach.

Of course it's right to push yourself to the maximum if you have a job to do or a specific goal to reach. The champion's model works, but not all of us are the 1%. We're not on social media, we're not the millionaire with a million euros to spend; even he has his problems. Different problems, but problems nonetheless.

Learn to do your own small process. For a while now I've been playing around with learning Spanish. I'm not forcing myself to learn this language: between work, dancing, Spanish and everything else I do, it could all become super heavy, and then you complain about having so many things to do. But if you remember to do that one thing for 15 minutes a day, or even skip a day, that's fine.

It's these small things that add up little by little, whether it's getting to know a person or learning a bit of Spanish, whether it's dance, salsa, tango, running, volleyball, the gym or theatre. Do it once a day, once a week, at whatever rhythm you want. But don't think "I'm doing this because I have to." Think "I'm doing this because I like it, because it interests me, because it stimulates my mind."

## 3. Small steps, every day

The third point is about small steps of progress in creativity, like in another post I wrote about the importance of being creative: making something new, something of your own, in your own environment. It can be a drawing you do in the evening, or, in my case, a website, a program or a little project.

Start doing it for yourself, because you like it and want to do it: a story you then share with someone, a drawing you send to someone, a piece of code you put online. You can do anything just for yourself, simply because you like it. And it doesn't even have to be a creative thing, or connected to anything else.

I dress up as a pirate and go running in the middle of the forest when it rains, because I like going dressed as a pirate in the forest. Why a pirate? Nothing, I just do it. And that, too, is something someone might like.

Don't think you're too strange or that you have strange tastes. We all do. Use a bit of common sense, of course, but many times the excuses we invent in our heads are absurd: "I won't do that lesson, because then I'll have to commit... wait, let me look at my phone for 15 minutes." Then "let me skip today, then tomorrow, then the day after", and then the guilt arrives. Yes, I'm a victim of this myself.

The important thing is not to beat yourself up for what you missed, but to try to do a little bit every day toward your goal, one small thing. Even recording this as an audio message and then transcribing it is something.

Do one small thing a day and things add up. People tend to overestimate what they can do in a short time and underestimate what they can do in a long time.
